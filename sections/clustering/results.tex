\subsection{Results}                     % what the clustering found
\label{sec:clust_results}                % \secref{sec:clust_results}

% \subsubsection{Overview and interpretation}  % main findings, before the detailed results
% \label{sec:clust_results_overview}       % \secref{sec:clust_results_overview}
In the following, we analyse and interpret only the K-means clustering ($K=3$) in detail,
for two reasons. First, three clusters are easier to compare between the registered and
unregistered latent codes, and to characterize morphologically, than the four clusters of the
agglomerative clustering. Second, K-means reaches a higher silhouette score than the
agglomerative clustering, so its clusters are better separated. The agglomerative results are
shown for completeness in Figures~\ref{fig:clust_agglo_proj_reg}
and~\ref{fig:clust_agglo_proj_unreg}.

% \paragraph{Structure of the latent space}  % what the projections show
For K-means, the latent codes of the unregistered trees yield a clearly better clustering than
those of the registered trees. On the registered codes, the three clusters (151, 200 and 68
trees) overlap heavily in all three projections (Figure~\ref{fig:clust_kmeans_proj_reg}). Only a
weak trend appears in UMAP, so K-means essentially partitions a homogeneous cloud. On the
unregistered codes, the clusters (140, 107 and 172 trees) are compact, balanced and spatially
coherent (Figure~\ref{fig:clust_kmeans_proj_unreg}). The same layout appears consistently in
kernel PCA, t-SNE and UMAP, and it agrees with the agglomerative clustering of the same codes
(Figure~\ref{fig:clust_agglo_proj_unreg}), which shows that the structure is robust both to the
projection and to the clustering algorithm.

Colouring the UMAP embedding by the tree-level metrics (Figure~\ref{fig:clust_metric_proj})
shows that the latent space is organized mainly by tree size and branching. Length, number of
branches, deepest generation, complexity and divergence are all consistently low along the left
tail and the upper-left edge of the embedding, where the smaller and simpler trees lie. These
metrics are likely correlated, so they probably reflect a single size and complexity factor.
Ectasia shows the most global gradient across the map and may act as a secondary axis, whereas
tortuosity and stenosis show no clear spatial pattern. No single metric explains the dense
central region, which suggests that the latent space also captures information beyond these
handcrafted descriptors.

For every tree, the eight descriptors are averaged over its branches. Comparing these eight values between clusters shows what distinguishes the groups, and colouring
the projections by each value shows whether the latent space is organized along these
anatomical properties.

Tables~\ref{tab:unreg-kmeans} to~\ref{tab:reg-agglo} give, for each method and each set of
latent codes, the eight metrics within every cluster: mean, standard deviation and variance. The shading of each cell shows the standardized difference $d$ of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:cluster_d}: red cells lie above the overall mean, blue cells below, and darker cells
further from it.

\paragraph{Summary matrices}             % how the two derived tables were built
To compare the clusters quantitatively, we derived two summary matrices from the per-cluster
statistics of the K-means clusters (Tables~\ref{tab:reg-kmeans} and~\ref{tab:unreg-kmeans}).
The column \emph{All} is identical in both tables: the metrics are computed on the trees
themselves and do not change with registration, only the cluster assignments do. The comparison
therefore shows by which morphological properties the latent space groups the trees, with and
without registration.

The first matrix (Table~\ref{tab:clust_d}) gives, for every cluster and metric, the
standardized difference between the cluster mean and the mean over all 419 trees,
\begin{equation}
  d = \frac{\bar{x}_{\text{cluster}} - \bar{x}_{\text{all}}}{s_{\text{all}}} ,
  \label{eq:cluster_d}                   % standardized difference, also used for the table shading
\end{equation}
where $s_{\text{all}}$ is the standard deviation over all trees. Expressing the difference in
units of the overall spread makes metrics with very different scales comparable, from tortuosity
values around 0.6 to branch counts above 200. The second matrix (Table~\ref{tab:clust_eta2})
ranks the metrics by how well they separate the clusters, using the effect size $\eta^2$, the
share of a metric's variance that is explained by cluster membership,
\begin{equation}
  \eta^2 = \frac{\sum_{k} n_k \left(\bar{x}_k - \bar{x}_{\text{all}}\right)^2}
                {\sum_{i} \left(x_i - \bar{x}_{\text{all}}\right)^2}
         = \frac{1}{N-1} \sum_{k} n_k\, d_k^2 ,
  \label{eq:eta2}                        % between-cluster share of the variance
\end{equation}
where $n_k$ and $d_k$ are the size and the standardized difference of cluster $k$, and
$N = 419$. Both quantities can therefore be computed from the cluster sizes, means and overall
standard deviation alone. As a common rule of thumb, $\eta^2 \approx 0.01$ is a small effect,
$0.06$ a medium one and $0.14$ or more a large one \cite{cohen1988}. The table also gives the
largest gap between two cluster means, in units of $s_{\text{all}}$.

% \paragraph{Interpretation}               % projections and matrices read together
With registration, the clusters fall along a single size and complexity axis
(Table~\ref{tab:clust_d}). Cluster~0 groups the larger and more complex trees, with
above-average length ($d = +0.45$), complexity ($+0.41$), divergence ($+0.40$), ectasia
($+0.36$) and number of branches ($+0.35$). Cluster~1 groups smaller and simpler trees, with
below-average values for the same metrics ($-0.18$ to $-0.35$), and cluster~2 lies close to the
average for all of them, differing only by a slightly lower tortuosity ($-0.33$) and a higher
stenosis ($+0.32$). Accordingly, length, complexity and divergence are the most discriminative
metrics ($\eta^2 = 0.130$, $0.107$ and $0.095$, Table~\ref{tab:clust_eta2}), followed by the
number of branches and ectasia, while tortuosity, stenosis and the deepest generation have only
small effects ($\eta^2 \le 0.03$). No metric reaches a large effect, which matches the strong
overlap of the registered clusters in the projections.

Without registration, the clusters differ mainly in airway shape. Cluster~1 is defined by a
clearly lower tortuosity ($d = -0.77$) and ectasia ($-0.70$), together with a shorter length
($-0.47$) and a slightly higher stenosis ($+0.26$), while its number of branches is exactly
average ($d = 0.00$). Cluster~2 again groups the larger and more complex trees, with
above-average ectasia ($+0.41$), length ($+0.35$), complexity ($+0.29$) and divergence
($+0.25$), so a group of large, complex trees is found in both conditions (registered cluster~0,
unregistered cluster~2). Tortuosity and ectasia are now the most discriminative metrics
($\eta^2 = 0.206$ and $0.195$), the only large effects in either condition, with cluster means
about 1.1 standard deviations apart. Length comes a distant third ($\eta^2 = 0.107$), and the
topological metrics contribute little (number of branches $0.029$, deepest generation $0.012$).

Registration therefore changes what the latent space encodes. After registration, the clusters
follow mostly the global size and branching of the trees with differences in curvature
and calibre no longer separate the groups. Without registration, the latent space captures both
size and airway geometry, which gives the strongest single-metric separations in either
condition and fits the observation that the unregistered clusters are more distinct and more
interpretable. Moreover, even the most discriminative
metric explains only about 20\% of the variance, so no single metric defines the clusters.
